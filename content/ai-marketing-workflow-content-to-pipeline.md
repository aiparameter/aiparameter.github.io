Many teams adopt AI by producing more content faster. Output volume is easy to measure and a poor proxy for results. A better approach starts with the whole workflow, from research to revenue, and asks which steps a model can improve and how you would know.

## Map the workflow first

List the stages of your current process in order. A typical marketing workflow looks like this:

1. Audience and keyword research
2. Briefing
3. Drafting
4. Editing and fact-checking
5. Design and formatting
6. Distribution
7. Lead capture and follow-up
8. Reporting

For each stage, write who does it, how long it takes and what goes wrong. This map is the baseline. Without it, you cannot tell whether AI helped.

## Decide where a model drafts and a person decides

Anthropic’s guidance on building with models separates predictable multi-step workflows from open-ended agents, and recommends the simpler option when it is enough. Marketing usually benefits from the simpler design: a fixed sequence in which a model produces a draft at a defined step and a person approves before anything is published.

Good candidates for model assistance:

- Turning a brief into a first draft or several variations
- Summarizing research notes and call transcripts
- Reformatting one asset into other formats
- Classifying inbound leads or support questions
- Drafting follow-up messages for review

Poor candidates, at least without close review:

- Factual claims, statistics and quotations
- Anything that makes a legal, medical or financial promise
- Positioning decisions and brand voice
- Messages sent automatically without review

## Evidence to keep in proportion

Research on generative AI in a customer-support setting, published as an NBER working paper, found productivity gains among agents using an assistant, with the largest gains for less experienced workers. That is evidence about customer support, not about marketing. It is a reason to test in your own setting, not a forecast of what you will see.

## Measure pipeline, not output

Choose measures that sit near revenue:

- Time from brief to approved draft
- Edit rate: how much of the draft survives review
- Qualified leads per piece, and cost per qualified lead
- Conversion from lead to opportunity, compared with a pre-AI period
- Error rate caught in review

Run a controlled comparison where you can. Have half of a content series follow the new process and half the old, and compare after a full cycle.

## Protect quality and trust

Set rules before scale. Require a named person to approve every published piece. Keep a source list for every factual claim. Decide how you will disclose AI assistance where your audience or a regulator expects it. Review a sample of published work each month for errors and drift in tone.

## A first experiment

Pick one stage, such as turning webinar transcripts into follow-up emails. Time the current process for two weeks, introduce a model-drafted step with human approval for the next two, and compare time and response rates. If the gain is real, extend to the next stage. If it is not, you have learned that cheaply.
